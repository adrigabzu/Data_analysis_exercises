Guide for participants

Participatory Systems Mapping with quantitative variables

A few promises before we start:

  • There is no right answer and nothing to hand in.
  • Everyone's perspective is useful. Complexity arises from your different views.
  • Bring your curiosity, and a willingness to say "wait, why?" out loud.

Marked text is the quantitative cue: the place where a factor becomes something you could measure, count or test.

Why we are doing this

In a lot of cases in quantitative analysis we test an exposure against an outcome and adjust everything else. In fact, we often assume that the effect of each exposure is independent of the others. Interactions between all those variables that are not the outcome are rarely tested.

A systems map is the opposite move. It puts all the factors on one page and makes the connections between them the object of study. What you build in this exercise is a qualitative map with quantifiable factors around a health challenge of interest.

The exercise step by step

For the next 35 minutes you will build a map of a health problem with people who might not think exactly like you. That is the whole point.

  1. 1. What interests you, and which problem? · 5 min

    You hopefully know each other by now so just remind your name to the group. Then each person shares in maximum ONE minute which aspects of the health problem they are interested in.

    Then the group chooses one problem to map (hopefully the one they are working on) and writes it in the centre of the page. If you want to try a new one and you want some ideas, here are a few:

    • Colorectal cancer is rising among individuals younger than 50
    • Access to cardiovascular disease prevention is socially unequal
    • Vaping and consumption of nicotine products among adolescents is on the rise

    Who facilitates?

    Whoever in the group has the first name earliest in the alphabet becomes the facilitator and timekeeper. We are in the land of good clocks and watches, so we trust you.

    Open the facilitator's guide Only the facilitator needs this page.

  2. 2. Brainstorm factors · 5 min

    Working individually, write at least three factors that could be involved in that problem, one factor per post-it.

    Try to write variables that can in some sense go up or down, such as "physical exercise", "funding" or "trust in services", rather than fixed labels such as "the health system".

  3. 3. Merge and connect · 20 min

    Put your post-its around the central problem. If two factors are the same or nearly the same, the people who wrote them decide together whether to keep just one.

    Discuss which factors influence each other and draw arrows in one direction between them. Mark each arrow:

    • (+)when the factors reinforce each other, meaning both move in the same direction.
    • (-)when one suppresses the other, meaning one goes up and the other goes down.
    • ?when an arrow feels genuinely unclear, or when the honest answer is "it depends".
    • A second arrow backA second arrow back when the relation is reciprocal.

    (+) means same direction, not good. More smoking leading to more lung cancer is a (+) arrow.

  4. 4. Wrap up the map!· 5 min

    Keep drawing arrows and refining until the facilitator calls time.

    Circle the two or three connections your group would most want to test if you had the data.

Then we reconvene as one group for 10 minutes of discussion.

What the map is, and what it is not

It is a shared picture of how your group thinks the factors relate, built from your own knowledge and from the disagreements you had while drawing it.

It is not an estimate of an effect, not a validated model, and not the output of an analysis. The boundary of the map, meaning what you put inside it and what you left out, is a choice your group made.

An unfinished map is the normal state of a systems map. Nobody finishes one, and the question of what is missing is part of the result.

Emergent voices · 10 min

By the end we reconvene as one group and discuss what came out of the maps. Keep these prompts in mind:

  • What did you like about this exercise, and what was the most challenging part?
  • Did you learn something new about the problem, or about how another discipline frames it?
  • Where did you disagree in your group, and did you resolve it? Share the unresolved ones, those are the good ones to discuss.
  • Whose perspective is missing from this map, and whose factor would we have written differently?
